Understanding Wavelength, Energy, and Wavenumber Conversion

Why convert wavelength to energy?

Wavelength (nm), photon energy (eV), wavenumber (cm⁻¹), and frequency (THz) are four ways of describing the same electromagnetic wave. Spectroscopists, laser engineers, and photonics researchers switch between them constantly: laser manufacturers specify wavelength, chemists reading infrared or Raman spectra work in wavenumber, semiconductor physicists talk about bandgap energy in eV, and RF or telecom engineers think in frequency. Converting between them by hand is error-prone, so a dedicated wavelength to energy converter saves time and avoids unit mistakes in publications and lab notebooks.

The core formula: E = hc/λ

Photon energy is given by E = hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant and c is the speed of light. Expressed in the units researchers use most often, this simplifies to E(eV) = 1239.84198 / λ(nm) — a constant worth memorizing. Wavenumber follows a similar reciprocal relationship: ν̃(cm⁻¹) = 1 / λ(cm), which becomes ν̃(cm⁻¹) = 1e7 / λ(nm) when wavelength is given in nanometers. Frequency is simplest of all: f = c / λ, or f(THz) = 299792.458 / λ(nm).

Why does wavelength compression happen when converting to energy scaling?

Because energy, wavenumber, and frequency are all inversely proportional to wavelength, equal steps in wavelength do not produce equal steps in energy. Near the visible spectrum's blue end, a 10 nm change in wavelength corresponds to a much larger jump in eV than the same 10 nm change near the infrared end. This nonlinear "compression" is why spectra plotted on an energy or wavenumber axis look stretched at short wavelengths and squeezed at long wavelengths compared to the same data plotted on a linear wavelength axis. It also explains why UV and X-ray photons carry disproportionately more energy per nanometer of wavelength shift than infrared photons do — the inverse relationship amplifies energy differences as wavelength shrinks.

Choosing the right unit for your field

Electron-volts (eV) are the standard in semiconductor physics and photodiode/laser diode datasheets, since bandgap energies are naturally expressed this way. Wavenumber (cm⁻¹) dominates infrared, Raman, and vibrational spectroscopy because it is directly proportional to energy and vibrational frequencies of molecular bonds are conventionally tabulated in cm⁻¹. Frequency (THz or Hz) is preferred in telecommunications, ultrafast optics, and anywhere signal bandwidth matters. Wavelength (nm or µm) remains the most intuitive unit for describing color, laser output, and optical filter specifications. This calculator lets you enter a value in any one of these four units and see the other three update immediately, so you never need to memorize which multiplier to use.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you convert wavelength to energy in eV?

E(eV) = 1239.84198 / λ(nm). A 632.8 nm HeNe laser photon is about 1.959 eV.

What is the formula for wavelength to wavenumber conversion?

ν̃(cm⁻¹) = 1e7 / λ(nm). A 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser is about 9398.5 cm⁻¹.

Why does energy increase as wavelength decreases?

Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength (E = hc/λ), so shorter wavelengths pack more oscillation cycles per unit length — higher frequency, higher energy.

What is the photon energy of a 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser?

Approximately 1.165 eV, 9398.5 cm⁻¹, and 281.76 THz.

How do you convert energy in eV back to wavelength in nm?

λ(nm) = 1239.84198 / E(eV) — the same constant, applied in reverse.

How do I compare multiple absorption spectra on one chart?

Click "Add spectrum" to overlay another trace with its own auto-assigned color and legend label, loaded from an example, an uploaded CSV, or pasted data.

How do I make a waterfall-style stacked spectrum plot?

Set a nonzero Y offset in a spectrum's row so overlapping curves shift vertically apart for readability.

Can I export a spectrum as a vector SVG for a publication figure?

Yes — "Download chart (SVG)" exports a vector file that opens cleanly in Illustrator or Inkscape, alongside the PNG export.

What is a colorblind-safe palette and why does it matter?

A set of colors, like the Okabe-Ito 8-color palette, that stay distinguishable under common color vision deficiencies — poor color choices are one of editors' most-cited figure-quality complaints, so new spectra use this palette by default.

How do I add a closed axis box like GraphPad Prism?

Open "Customize chart" and check "Closed axis box" to add the top/right border and mirrored tick marks, matching the convention many biology and chemistry journals expect.

Can I upload one CSV with multiple sample spectra at once?

Yes — a file with wavelength in column 1 and several sample columns after it automatically splits into one spectrum per column, using the column headers as legend labels if present.

Can I plot steady-state fluorescence excitation or emission spectra?

Yes — set "Spectrum type" to "Fluorescence (steady-state)" and the Y axis relabels to Fluorescence Intensity (a.u.); overlay, offsets, colors, and export all work the same as absorption mode.

Can I download the data exactly as shown on the chart?

Yes — "Download data (CSV)" exports only the currently visible spectra, with offsets applied and cropped to any custom X-axis range you've set.

Yes — "Save project (.json)" downloads every loaded spectrum plus your chart settings as one local file; "Load project" reads it straight back in later, on any device, with no account or server involved.
